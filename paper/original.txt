ABSTRACT
Formative assessment plays a pivotal role in adaptive education by continuously evaluating a
learner’s knowledge evolution to guide personalized instruction. Effective knowledge tracing models are essential for this purpose, enabling educational systems to infer student understanding
from historical interaction data. While recurrent neural network (RNN)-based approaches such
as Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) have advanced this field, they often encounter scalability and
interpretability challenges when modeling long interaction sequences. Transformer-based methods,
including Self-Attentive Knowledge Tracing (SAKT), have improved performance through attention
mechanisms but suffer from quadratic computational complexity, limiting real-time deployment.
In this work, we propose LightSAKT, a lightweight and computationally efficient self-attentive
knowledge tracing framework that integrates a linear attention mechanism to reduce complexity from O(n²) to O(n). The proposed architecture preserves the interpretability and contextual
reasoning of attention-based models while substantially lowering memory and runtime overhead.
Experimental evaluations on three benchmark datasets—ASSISTments 2009, ASSISTments 2015,
and Algebra 2005—demonstrate that LightSAKT consistently outperforms existing models such as
DKT, DKVMN, and SAKT in terms of AUC, accuracy, and F1 score. These findings confirm that
LightSAKT delivers competitive predictive accuracy with enhanced scalability, making it a viable
solution for real-time formative assessment and large-scale intelligent tutoring systems. The findings highlight the potential of Transformer architectures in revolutionizing formative assessment
methodologies, paving the way for more effective educational technologies.
Keywords: Formative assessment, Knowledge Tracing; Self-attention; Skill Mastery; Personalized
Learning; Computer-aided Learning.
1 Introduction
Computer-aided learning solutions such as intelligent tutoring and learning management systems
have recently emerged to complement or replace the teacher in traditional classroom teaching
[1]. The enormous societal benefits of online education are a direct result of its ability to overcome
conventional classroom instruction’s geographical and temporal constraints [2, 3]. As students learn
at different rates and retain information at different rates of depth, catering to each student’s unique
needs is the biggest challenge in technology-enhanced teaching. Formative assessments [4] involve
various activities carried out during the learning process to track student progress and help students
improve before final evaluations. These assessments are usually quizzes, discussions, assignments,
projects, or other tasks that provide insight into a student’s understanding and learning needs
[5]. Knowledge tracing (KT) can be a valuable tool for formative assessments that involve the
machine-based modeling of a student’s knowledge as they engage with their coursework [6].
Knowledge Tracing (KT) is a sequential modeling task that aims to estimate a student’s evolving
mastery of knowledge concepts by analyzing their historical interaction data, typically consisting ofexercise attempts and correctness feedback. Early deep learning approaches such as Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) [Piech et al., 2015] employed Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to capture
temporal dependencies within student interactions. While effective in modeling sequential data,
RNN-based models often suffer from limitations including the vanishing gradient problem, difficulty in capturing long-range dependencies, and a lack of interpretability, especially when applied
to sparse or irregular educational datasets.
The Transformer architecture itself revolutionized sequence modeling by leveraging multi-head
self-attention and positional encoding to capture both local and global dependencies while enabling
parallelized training. Despite these advantages, its quadratic complexity remains a challenge in
scaling to long sequences. Consequently, several efficient transformer variants have been proposed
to mitigate this issue. Linear Transformers [Katharopoulos et al., 2020] and Performer [Choromanski et al., 2021] introduced kernel-based attention mechanisms with O(n) complexity using
feature map approximations. Similarly, the Linformer [Wang et al., 2020] reduces complexity by
projecting keys and values into a low-dimensional space, and the Reformer [Kitaev et al., 2020]
applies locality-sensitive hashing to approximate attention on long sequences. These models significantly enhance the scalability of attention mechanisms without severely compromising model
performance. Motivated by the success of efficient attention mechanisms, we propose LightSAKT, a
lightweight variant of SAKT that incorporates linear attention for reduced computational overhead.
By replacing scaled dot-product attention with a kernelized attention mechanism and simplifying
the feed-forward layers, LightSAKT achieves lower time and memory complexity while maintaining competitive predictive performance. This makes it suitable for large-scale knowledge tracing
tasks and deployment in resource-constrained environments, such as real-time intelligent tutoring
systems.

Formative assessment can be accomplished with the help of knowledge tracing as shown in
Figure 2, representing formative assessment cycle. Several Knowledge-tracing models for formative
assessment that use statistical and machine-learning methods have been proposed by researchers
over the past 20 years [8]. Hidden Markov models (HMMs) and Bayesian Knowledge Tracing
(BKT) models [9] were the first models in this domain. However, these models are oversimplified,
have less accuracy, and cannot capture the evolution of students’ mastery of skills. Recently,
many researchers have utilized deep neural networks to create knowledge-tracing models such as
Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) [10] with RNN and DKT+ [11] with LSTMs. However, these
models have issues like overfitting and are not quite successful in capturing the memory retention
behavior of a student [12]. These models also struggle to capture long-term dependencies and
complex patterns in long sequence education data. In addition, they often exhibit inconsistency
in prediction. Moreover, LSTM-based DKT may also suffer from the vanishing gradient problem.
To overcome these issues, this work proposes the integration of self-attention-based transformers
[13] with a deep knowledge tracing model. The self-attention-based models can make predictions
using all the historical student interaction data. Moreover, these models prevent overfitting, giving
better performance.

42.1 Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT)
The Bayesian knowledge tracing (BKT) model, introduced in 1994 [7], offers a framework for modeling the knowledge states of knowledge components (KCs). In this model, each KC is represented
by a Hidden Markov Model (HMM), where the hidden state denotes the student’s level of mastery
over the KC. This hidden state serves as an indicator, signaling whether the KC is mastered or not
[9].
But there are a few unrealistic presumptions in BKT. By treating KCs as separate entities,
BKT, for instance, assumes that forgetting is not happening. Researchers have developed BKT
versions, including models that consider skill dependencies and forgetting mechanisms, to solve
these drawbacks [22, 23]. Enhancements to BKT have also been made by integrating partial
scores [24], sub-skills, and temporal attributes [25]. Nonetheless, it is important to note that these
extensions often require extensive feature engineering and can significantly increase computational
complexity.
2.2 Learning Factors Analysis (LFA)
In the 2000s, Learning Factors Analysis (LFA) [26] emerged as an alternative approach for modeling
student knowledge states. LFA employed a logistic regression model to tackle the issue of multiple
KCs and to incorporate student ability into the model. LFA [15] is a semi-automated technique
that enhances cognitive models by integrating a statistical model, expert human knowledge, and
combinatorial search methods. Fundamentally, its statistical model functions similarly to a KT
model. LFA is derived from the learning curve concept, which identifies a power-law relationship
between error rates and practice frequency. Unlike traditional learning curves, which don’t account
for the diversity of learners and the multifaceted nature of questions involving multiple skills, LFA
modifies and expands upon this concept to address these complexities effectively.
2.3 Performance Factors Analysis (PFA)
A reconfiguration of LFA, termed Performance Factors Analysis (PFA) [16], focuses more on student
performance rather than exclusively on student ability, enhancing sensitivity to performance-related
factors. PFA [15] posits that each learner’s journey is distinct, recognizing that learning is not solely
a function of practice frequency. Instead, PFA acknowledges that learners accumulate knowledge
through both their correct and incorrect attempts, highlighting the individual nature of the learning
process.
LFA and PFA utilize the count of successes or failures in applying a knowledge component to
predict whether a student has mastered the corresponding knowledge. While these methods can
handle tasks involving multiple KCs, they do not address the inherent dependencies among KCs.
For instance, understanding ”multiplication” requires knowledge of ”addition.” Additionally, the
features employed in LFA and PFA are relatively simple, lacking the depth to comprehensively
understand students’ latent knowledge states.
2.4 Dynamic Key-Value Memory Networks for Knowledge Tracing (DKVMN)
DKVMN represents a significant advancement, offering a mechanism to gauge a student’s mastery
across various concepts by capitalizing on the interconnectedness between them. Unlike conventional memory-augmented neural networks, DKVMN [17] adopts a novel architecture comprising
two matrices: a static ”key” matrix storing knowledge concepts and a dynamic ”value” matrix
tracking and updating mastery levels associated with each concept. This dynamic adaptation facilitates a real-time understanding of student proficiency, catering to the dynamic nature of learning.
However, DKVMN encounters certain limitations. Its reliance on explicit concept relationships
may not accurately capture student learning behavior’s complexities. Additionally, DKVMN’s
5performance may be hindered by its static key matrix, which could struggle to accommodate the
evolving complexities of educational data.
2.5 Exercise-Aware Knowledge Tracing (EKT)
One notable approach to predicting student performance in computer-supported intelligent education systems is the Exercise-Enhanced Recurrent Neural Network (EERNN) framework [27]. This
model leverages students’ exercise records and the exercises’ textual content to provide a more holistic prediction of student performance. The EERNN framework integrates a bidirectional LSTM to
encode the content of each exercise and summarizes each student’s state into an integrated vector.
Additionally, the framework extends to an Exercise-aware Knowledge Tracing (EKT) model [19]
that incorporates knowledge concept information. In EKT, a memory network is developed to
quantify the impact of each exercise on students’ mastery of various knowledge concepts, allowing
for an interpretable analysis of knowledge acquisition.
2.6 Graph-based knowledge tracing (GKT)
GKT applies graph neural networks (GNNs) to the field of KT [20]. It involves predicting student
performance on coursework exercises over time, which can be represented as a graph in terms of
data structure. Integrating the graph-structured nature into the knowledge-tracing model as a
relational bias can enhance performance. Relational bias, in this context, refers to the intentional
integration of relational information or dependencies between different entities within the data into
the model architecture. These relationships can be explicit (e.g., prerequisites between skills) or
implicit (e.g., patterns in student interactions), providing valuable context for making predictions.
2.7 Deep Learning-based Techniques
The groundbreaking work in knowledge tracing by many researchers has demonstrated the efficacy
of deep learning methods [28, 29] in knowledge tracing [30]. A model known as Deep Knowledge
Tracing (DKT) makes use of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) [31] to record the temporal
dynamics of a series of interactions between a student’s responses to questions and their answers
and then predicts the student’s response to a subsequent question. Empirical findings demonstrated
that DKT performed better on multiple benchmark datasets than conventional KT models. Many
recent works have taken use of the advancements in deep learning KT models in recent years,
examining them from many angles, such as textual characteristics [32], forgetting features [33],
memory structures [34], and attention mechanisms [35].
2.8 Transformer-based Techniques
In [35], self attention based model for knowledge tracing has been proposed. The suggested approach, known by the acronym SAKT, predicts a student’s success on a given exercise by allocating
weights to previously completed exercises. In order to directly diagnose the learner’s knowledge
proficiency from each question mastery state, the authors in [36] proposed a model named as
DTransformer by designing the architecture from the question level to the knowledge level. In
order to preserve the stability of the knowledge state diagnosis, the authors also provided a unique
contrastive learning (CL)-based training approach. Without depending on extreme details, the
models can learn high-level intrinsics in final representations with the use of contrastive learning.
2.9 Light-weight Techniques
To tackle scalability issues, researchers have proposed lightweight KT architectures. LightKT
(Wang et al., 2021) pruned redundant model parameters and introduced a knowledge-aware design
6to reduce training overhead. SKVM (Shin et al., 2021) employed sparse memory access and compression techniques to minimize memory usage. These models, although computationally efficient,
still often depended on traditional attention mechanisms or customized memory networks. To overcome the quadratic bottleneck of self-attention, Linear Transformers (Katharopoulos et al., 2020)
proposed a kernel-based attention mechanism using the ELU + 1 transformation to approximate
softmax. This reduced attention complexity from O(n²) to O(n). Other efficient attention models include: Performer (Choromanski et al., 2021): uses FAVOR+ for randomized feature maps.
Reformer (Kitaev et al., 2020): utilizes locality-sensitive hashing (LSH). Linformer (Wang et al.,
2020): projects key and value matrices to lower dimensions.
These models proved effective in long-sequence NLP tasks, inspiring their adoption in knowledge
tracing contexts.

3.2 Data Preprocessing
All datasets were preprocessed to ensure consistency and compatibility with the proposed LightSAKT model. For each dataset, only the essential attributes—student identifier, problem identifier, and response correctness—were retained. Records containing missing values or duplicates
were removed. All identifiers were re-indexed into contiguous integer values starting from zero,
and response outcomes were normalized to binary labels, where 1 denotes a correct response and
0 denotes an incorrect response.
For each student, interaction records were sorted chronologically to form a learning sequence
represented as:
X = {(e1, r1),(e2, r2), . . . ,(et, rt)}, (1)
where ei denotes the exercise identifier at time step i and ri ∈ {0, 1} indicates the corresponding
response correctness.
To obtain fixed-length inputs for mini-batch training, sequences longer than 100 interactions
for the ASSISTments 2009 and 2015 datasets and 200 interactions for the Algebra 2005 dataset
were truncated. Sequences shorter than these thresholds were padded with zeros. A binary mask
tensor m was constructed to indicate valid sequence positions, ensuring that padded entries did
not contribute to the loss computation or evaluation metrics.
To distinguish correct and incorrect attempts for the same exercise, each interaction was encoded using:
yi = ei + ri × E, (2)
where E represents the total number of unique exercises in the dataset. This encoding follows the
model’s internal representation scheme x = q + numq × r, enabling the embedding layer to learn
distinct latent representations for correct and incorrect responses to the same exercise.
During preprocessing, aligned tensors were generated for model training, including the current
exercise sequence q, the corresponding response correctness sequence r, the shifted exercise sequence
qshft, the shifted response labels rshft, and the binary mask m. The shifted sequences represent the
next-step prediction targets for each time step.
To prevent information leakage, all datasets were partitioned at the student level into 90%
training and 10% testing subsets. The resulting sequences were converted into PyTorch tensors
and organized into mini-batches of size 64 using custom data loaders. Each mini-batch provided
the model with the tuple:
(q, r, qshft, rshft, m), (3)
9which ensures full alignment between the preprocessing pipeline and the model’s forward computation.
Feature Engineering
Feature selection was applied manually to extract relevant information and create informative
features for model training. In the ASSIST2009 dataset, after preprocessing, the following features
were selected for model training and evaluation:
• User ID (user id): Each unique user ID represents a distinct student in the dataset. User
IDs were utilized to track individual student performance and learning patterns.
• Skill Name (skill name): The skill name column contains the identifiers for different
knowledge components (skills) assessed in the dataset. These skills play a pivotal role in
predicting student performance and mastery levels.
• Correctness of Response (correct): The correctness column indicates whether a student’s
response to a particular skill was correct or incorrect. This binary feature serves as the target
variable for training predictive models.
The same three features were extracted in the preprocessing steps for the ASSIST2015 and
Algebra2005 datasets, ensuring consistency in feature selection across different datasets and facilitating comprehensive model training and evaluation. These selected features provide valuable
insights into student behavior, skill acquisition, and learning progression.
Data Sequencing and Indexing It focuses on structuring the selected features into sequences
the model can process and converting categorical data into numerical indices. Sequencing user interactions involves organizing the interaction data (problems and responses) into sequential order
for each user, ensuring the temporal sequence of events is maintained. Indexing categorical features,
such as user IDs and skill names, is achieved by creating mappings and converting these categorical
values into integers. Sequences of problem identifiers and corresponding response correctness are
generated for each user. Finally, the processed sequences and mappings are serialized and saved
using pickle files, facilitating efficient data loading in future runs. Data Sequencing and Indexing
are crucial for preparing the data in a structured format that sequence models like RNNs or Transformers can process, ensuring compatibility with the model’s input requirements and preserving
the temporal context of the data for accurate modeling of student learning and prediction of future
performance.

designed for sequential student interaction data. LightSAKT incorporates embedding layers, a
linear multi-head attention mechanism, residual connections, a compact feed-forward network, and
a robust training strategy with hyperparameter tuning. This methodology elaborates on each
architectural and functional component as implemented, closely adhering to our implementation
strategy.

5 Results
5.1 Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol
In this research, the proposed LightSAKT model is evaluated on three publicly available knowledge
tracing datasets: ASSIST2009, ASSIST2015, and Algebra2005–2006. These datasets consist of
sequential student interaction records, where each interaction is represented by a question identifier
and a corresponding binary response indicating whether the question was answered correctly.
Data Splitting Strategy
For each dataset, student interactions were divided into training, validation, and test sets using
a session-wise splitting strategy to preserve the sequential nature of student learning behavior.
Specifically, 70% of the data was used for training, 10% for validation, and the remaining 20% for
testing. This splitting strategy ensures that student interaction sequences in the test set remain unseen during training, thereby providing a fair and realistic evaluation of the model’s generalization
capability.
Model Configuration
The LightSAKT architecture comprises an interaction embedding layer, a learnable positional
encoding, and a linear multi-head attention mechanism, followed by residual connections and a
feed-forward network. This configuration enables efficient modeling of long-range dependencies in
student interaction sequences while maintaining linear computational complexity.
13Training Details
All models were trained using the Adam optimizer. The binary cross-entropy loss function was
employed, as the task involves predicting the probability of a correct response, which is inherently
a binary classification problem. The maximum number of training epochs was set to 50 for all
datasets. To prevent overfitting, early stopping was applied based on validation AUC with a fixed
patience, and the model achieving the best validation performance was selected for final evaluation.
Evaluation Metrics
The performance of the proposed model was assessed using standard evaluation metrics commonly
adopted in knowledge tracing literature:
• Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), to measure the ranking
quality of predictions;
• Accuracy (ACC), to evaluate overall prediction correctness;
• F1-score, to balance precision and recall under class imbalance;
• Mean Squared Error (MSE), to assess the quality of probabilistic predictions.
Computational Environment
All experiments were conducted using the PyTorch deep learning framework. Due to differences
in dataset scale, the computational environment varied across datasets. Experiments on the ASSIST2015 dataset were conducted using an NVIDIA H100 GPU to accommodate its larger size. For
the ASSIST2009 and Algebra2005–2006 datasets, model training and evaluation were performed
on an NVIDIA A100 GPU.

5.5 Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test
The Wilcoxon signed-rank test [41] was used to see if there was a statistically significant difference
between the two matched sets of observations (n = 11) which consisted of AUC values of TransDKT+ and baseline DKT+ on the same set of hyperparameters. The null hypothesis assumes
that the two sets are not different. This non-parametric test was chosen since it does not assume
normality and is acceptable for comparing matched samples. The findings showed a Wilcoxon
test statistic W = 66.0 with a p-value of 0.00049. The null hypothesis that the median difference between the two groups is zero is rejected at a significance threshold of alpha = 0.05, since
p < 0.05. These findings show a statistically substantial and consistent improvement in the measures matched, implying that the difference does not appear to be caused by random fluctuation.

5.8 Discussion
Compared to recurrent models such as DKT and DKT+, LightSAKT exhibits a clear advantage
in modeling long-term dependencies within student–exercise sequences. RNN-based architectures
often suffer from vanishing gradients and limited memory capacity. Although DKT+ incorporates
reconstruction loss for improved generalization, it remains computationally demanding. By employing a linear attention mechanism with O(n) complexity, LightSAKT effectively models contextual
dependencies without the degradation in performance observed in recurrent models.
When compared to memory-augmented models such as DKVMN, LightSAKT attains higher
predictive accuracy with significantly fewer parameters. While DKVMN enhances interpretability
through dual memory matrices, it incurs higher training time and resource costs. The lightweight
feed-forward design and kernel-based attention in LightSAKT reduce parameter count and enable
faster convergence across datasets, as reflected in its lower average training loss.
In contrast to attention-based models like SAKT and GKT, LightSAKT retains the interpretability benefits of attention mechanisms while improving scalability. SAKT’s full self-attention
incurs O(n²) computational cost, which limits its applicability to long sequences. GKT captures
graph-based relationships but depends on predefined graph structures, which are often unavailable in practice. LightSAKT eliminates this dependency by dynamically identifying relationships
21between exercises through linear attention, ensuring scalability to large datasets and extended
learning sessions.
B. Dataset-Wise Observations
Dataset-specific trends further emphasize the adaptability of LightSAKT On the ASSIST2015
dataset—characterized by sparse and short interaction sequences—LightSAKT achieves the largest
performance gain, improving AUC from 0.72 (SAKT) to approximately 0.98. This demonstrates
strong generalization in low-context environments. For the ASSIST2009 dataset, which is denser
and features longer sequences, LightSAKT effectively models long-range dependencies, surpassing
DKVMN and GKT in both accuracy and F1-score. On Algebra2005, which includes diverse skill
mappings and extended interaction histories, LightSAKT shows faster convergence and improved
stability, as depicted in Fig. 6, confirming its robustness across varying data densities and sequence
complexities.
C. Architectural Efficiency and Interpretability
The efficiency of LightSAKT results from the integration of linear attention, reduced hidden
dimensionality, and residual normalization. By halving the feed-forward hidden size and employing kernel-based attention, the model achieves significant memory and time reductions while preserving representational strength. Visualization of attention weights confirms that LightSAKT
accurately associates conceptually related exercises—even when they appear far apart—providing
interpretability comparable to SAKT at a fraction of the computational cost.
D. Practical Implications
The lightweight nature of LightSAKT makes it well-suited for deployment in formative assessment systems, intelligent tutoring platforms, and real-time learning analytics. Its O(n) computational complexity ensures scalability for continuous prediction and adaptive feedback. The model’s
interpretability facilitates integration with educational dashboards, enabling instructors to visualize
student mastery and knowledge progression effectively.
E. Limitations and Future Work
Despite its strong empirical performance, several limitations merit consideration. The current
evaluation assumes the presence of well-defined skill tags, whereas many educational datasets contain incomplete or noisy concept annotations. Extending LightSAKT to handle multi-skill and
unstructured interaction data remains a critical direction for future research. Additionally, while
linear attention ensures efficiency, it may underperform when modeling highly non-linear dependencies across disparate concepts. Future work will explore hybrid attention architectures that
adaptively combine full and linear attention mechanisms, as well as knowledge-graph–enhanced
variants to strengthen relational reasoning.
F. Summary
In summary, LightSAKT achieves a balanced trade-off between predictive accuracy, interpretability, and scalability. The model bridges the gap between heavy Transformer-based KT
methods and lightweight classical approaches through an efficient linear attention mechanism. The
findings highlight the potential of efficient Transformer variants in large-scale, real-time educational
settings, paving the way for the next generation of explainable and resource-efficient adaptive learning systems.
Performance Analysis The primary reason for LightSAKT’s enhanced predictive accuracy lies
in its foundation on the Transformer architecture, which is fundamentally more adept at capturing
complex temporal dependencies than recurrent models. 1. Superior Relationship Modeling with
Self-Attention: Unlike Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) such as LSTMs and GRUs employed
by DKT and GKT, which process sequences sequentially and are susceptible to difficulties in
retaining information over long intervals, the self-attention mechanism in LightSAKT evaluates
the influence of all past interactions simultaneously. For any given question (Query), the model
directly computes its relationship with all previous interactions (Keys) to synthesize a contextaware representation. This enables the model to identify and leverage long-range dependencies
that are critical for accurately modeling student knowledge acquisition but are often missed by
22sequential processing models.
2. Computational Efficiency via Linear Attention: A significant bottleneck of the standard
Transformer architecture is the quadratic computational and memory complexity, O(n2), of the
self-attention mechanism, where n is the sequence length. This complexity renders it impractical
for the long interaction sequences common in educational datasets. Our LightSAKT model integrates a Linear Attention module that circumvents this limitation. By leveraging the associative
property of matrix multiplication, it computes the attention output by rearranging the operation
from (QKT)V toQ(KT V ). This modification reduces the complexity to a linear scale, O(n), without creating the large intermediate n×n attention matrix. This efficiency allows LightSAKT to
process significantly longer student histories, thereby providing more context for its predictions
and improving model performance.
3. Improved Generalization with a Lightweight Feed-Forward Network (FFN): To further enhance model robustness and reduce the risk of overfitting, the FFN in LightSAKT is designed to be
parameter-efficient. By reducing the hidden layer’s dimensionality to half that of the model’s embedding dimension, we decrease the total parameter count. This architectural choice acts as a form
of regularization, compelling the model to learn more generalizable features of student learning
rather than memorizing the training data.
The study’s findings have significant implications in the real world for the creation of personalized education programs. The knowledge tracing leads to adaptive learning environment by
tracking learning progress of each student over time. The KT-based personalized education helps
in delivering timely and targeted feedback where students are able to observe how effectively they
comprehend and engage with the course material. Further the adaptive learning environment is
facilitated by allowing students to advance at their own speed because each student’s knowledge
status is monitored separately. In addition, with the use of formative feedback, students can identify
areas for improvement and potential support resources to modify learning practices to accomplish
course objectives. Moreover, the teachers can offer individualised attention and focused interventions to struggling students in one-on-one or class settings. This also helps educational institutions
to design curriculum and teacher training programs. Additionally, these models may be included in
already-existing Intelligent Tutoring Systems or Learning Management Systems to offer a scalable
learning environment.
Error Analysis
The suggested models exhibit exceptional performance; yet, some limits and error instances have
been identified. First, excessively long sequence lengths may increase memory utilisation without
yielding noticeable performance benefits, while short maximum sequence lengths may truncate
pertinent historical interactions. Second, the findings of the ablation study revealed worse performance when regularisation components, dropout or positional encoding were removed. Third, a
high learning rate may induce training instability. Fourth, attention weights had a tendency to
overfit for rare skills with few data, resulting in estimations of mastery that were unstable. Finally,
incorrect device setup might result in execution errors. For example, putting the model on CPU
when the input tensors are on GPU highlights the need for robust device handling.
By integrating Transformer-based modelling with an effective linear attention mechanism,
LightSAKT improves Knowledge Tracing and enables it to capture long-range relationships at
a lower computing cost. LightSAKT is appropriate for large-scale datasets because it achieves
quicker training and reduced memory use than SAKT, which has quadratic complexity. It gets
over LSTM drawbacks including disappearing gradients and poor long-term dependence modelling
in contrast to DKT and DKT+. In contrast to DKVMN, which uses static key-value memory,
LightSAKT represents changing concept relations dynamically. Additionally, it is more flexible
than GKT, which is reliant on set graph architectures. All things considered, LightSAKT offers a scalable, adaptable, and context-aware depiction of student learning behaviour, resulting in
increased precision and effectiveness when forecasting students’ knowledge levels.
236 Conclusion
Studying knowledge tracing as a formative assessment technique is a novel research field with only
a few existing studies in this area. However, existing models are oversimplified, have less accuracy, and cannot capture the evolution of students’ mastery of skills. Recently, many researchers
have utilized deep neural networks to create knowledge-tracing models. However, these models
have issues like overfitting, prediction inconsistency and are not quite successful in capturing the
memory retention behavior of a student. Moreover, existing models are memory hungry. Firstly,
our study has proposed a formative assessment framework for integrating transformers into deep
knowledge-tracing architecture for inferring student mastery of skills. Secondly, our work has
demonstrated that replacing the traditionally employed LSTMs with transformers has significantly
improved performance. Thirdly, our study has validated the performance of proposed TransDKT
and TransDKT+ methods with hyperparameter-tuned baselines DKT and DKT+ algorithms.
This study presented LightSAKT, a lightweight self-attentive framework for knowledge tracing,
designed to enhance the effectiveness of formative assessment in adaptive learning environments. By
integrating a linear attention mechanism within a transformer-inspired architecture, the proposed
model significantly reduces computational complexity while maintaining high predictive accuracy
and interpretability.
Experimental evaluations conducted on three benchmark datasets—ASSISTments 2009, ASSISTments 2015, and Algebra 2005—demonstrated that LightSAKT consistently outperforms conventional deep knowledge tracing models, including DKT, DKVMN, and SAKT, across key performance metrics such as AUC, F1 score, accuracy, and MSE. These results validate the scalability
and efficiency of the proposed approach for large-scale and real-time educational applications.
The findings highlight the potential of efficient transformer variants to balance accuracy, interpretability, and resource utilization in intelligent tutoring systems. Future research will focus
on extending this framework to multimodal knowledge tracing using contextual and behavioral
features, optimizing hyperparameters through metaheuristic algorithms, and deploying the model
in real-world classroom environments to support continuous, data-driven formative assessment.
Beyond measurable performance gains, the suggested approaches offer useful advantages for
adaptive learning systems. These methods provide personalized learning on a scale by providing
timely and tailored feedback, fine-grained tracking of student progress, and targeted instructor
interventions. However, these performance improvements come with certain trade-offs such as:
increased computational complexity, increased number of parameters, and requirement of extensive tuning. Future work entails employing a lightweight version of the transformer model and
employing metaheuristic techniques for hyperparameter optimization. Future work should also
include developing computer vision-based multimodal techniques that could model skill mastery
from students’ handwritten answer sheets, including diagrams and mathematical equations.
